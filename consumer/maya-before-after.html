Encouraging Credit Transparency

Redesigning credit visibility for everyday users

"Most banks show you your credit score.
They don't show you what it means."
This case study explores how surfacing the right credit signals — in plain language, at the right moment — changes how users relate to their financial health. The Before reflects a typical bank experience: data-dense, jargon-heavy, passive. The After is built around behavioural transparency: clarity, momentum, and actionable next steps.

Experimentation Design · Not a Fielded Test
Would the Redesign Actually Change Behaviour?
Everything above is the interaction design. What follows is how I'd validate it before shipping — a full A/B test design, a synthetic-but-honestly-computed result, and a ship decision that reasons about statistical and practical significance, not just a p-value. This experiment was not run on real users. The underlying population is simulated specifically for this case study, clearly labelled at every step below — same honesty pattern as the synthetic population on the Aeroplan piece. The hypothesis being tested here didn't come from nowhere — it's the direct output of the qualitative research synthesis that shaped the redesign in the first place.
Hypothesis

If we replace the dense, jargon-heavy credit report with a plain-language Credit Quick View — a visual score ring, on-time-payment and utilization stats stated in plain terms, and one concrete next action — then the rate at which users engage with a secondary credit element will increase, because removing jargon lowers the cognitive cost of engaging with the section, and a specific, quantified next action ("pay $200 to gain ~8 pts") gives users something to act on instead of a wall of undifferentiated data. This is the same behavioural argument made qualitatively above; the test below is how I'd check whether it actually holds.

Experiment Design
Control vs. Variant
50/50 split, randomized at the user level (not session) to avoid one person seeing both experiences across visits. Control = the existing dense report (Before). Variant = the plain-language Credit Quick View (After).
Primary Metric
Credit Engagement Rate — of users who view the Credit section in a session, the % who additionally interact with at least one secondary element (a menu item, a dropdown, or the next-action nudge) rather than just glancing at the score and leaving.
Guardrail 1
Credit-screen bounce rate — must not move adversely. A simplified screen that pushes people away faster would mean we removed detail people actually needed, not just jargon.
Guardrail 2
Next-action nudge dismissal rate (variant only) — must stay under a 25% acceptability ceiling, so the new nudge reads as useful, not as spam.
Sample Size / Minimum Detectable Effect

The sample size needed depends on three things: how big a lift we actually care about detecting, how confident we want to be that a result isn't noise, and how much natural variability exists in the baseline rate itself.

Confidence level95%(α = 0.05, two-tailed)
Statistical power80%chance of detecting the effect if it's real
Baseline rate (control)11.0%
Target rate (variant)13.2%+2.2pp, a 20% relative lift
→ Required sample size3,449 per arm — rounded to 3,500

The 11.0% baseline is a planning assumption for a secondary in-app feature in mobile banking — Maya is a composite/fictional product for this case study, so this isn't tied to one company's disclosed figure, it's a stated assumption, not a sourced one. The 13.2% target reflects internal product norms treating relative lifts under ~15% on a secondary engagement metric as not worth a design change's engineering cost — the test needs to reliably detect at least that much.

two-proportion sample size — standard pooled formula, α=0.05 two-tailed, power=80%
n = (zα/2·√(2p̄(1−p̄)) + zβ·√(p₁(1−p₁)+p₂(1−p₂)))² / (p₂−p₁)² z0.025 = 1.960 z0.80 = 0.8416 p₁ = 0.110 p₂ = 0.132 p̄ = 0.121 n = 3,449 per arm → planned 3,500 per arm (7,000 total), rounded up for a small buffer

Decision rule, set before looking at results: ship if the primary-metric lift is statistically significant at α=0.05 and neither guardrail moves adversely — a clean win on engagement that costs us bounce or trains people to ignore nudges isn't actually a win.

Synthetic dataset. No real users were tested. Significance Detail

In plain terms: the difference we saw is very unlikely to be random chance — there's roughly a 1-in-180 probability (p = 0.0056) of seeing a gap this large if the redesign actually did nothing. The true lift is most likely somewhere between +0.65pp and +3.75pp, and two independent tests (a z-test and a chi-squared test) agree with each other, which is a good cross-check that the result isn't a quirk of one particular method.

Statistical Significance

p=0.0056 clears the pre-registered α=0.05 threshold with room to spare. More useful than the p-value alone: the 95% confidence interval on the lift is [+0.65pp, +3.75pp] — even the low end of our uncertainty about the true effect is a genuine positive number, not a result that only looks significant because of exactly where the p-value line was drawn.

Practical Significance

A 2.2pp absolute lift is a real number, but "statistically real" and "worth shipping" are different questions. Scaled against an assumed 42,000 monthly credit-section viewers — an illustrative figure for this exercise, not a disclosed metric — a 2.2pp lift works out to roughly 924 additional engaged sessions per month. Three things push this from "modest" to "worth it": the change is a UI and copy redesign of an existing screen, not new infrastructure, so the cost to ship is low and a 19% relative lift clears that bar easily; both guardrails are clean, meaning the lift isn't quietly costing us bounce or training people to swipe past nudges; and the direction and mechanism match the stated hypothesis — jargon removal plus a concrete next action — rather than being an unexplained anomaly that happens to be significant.

Caveats

A single simulated run — synthetic or real — is not proof of the true population effect; it's one draw from a distribution, which is exactly why the confidence interval matters more than the point estimate. In a real fielded version of this test I'd also worry about a novelty effect: interaction redesigns often get an initial curiosity-driven engagement bump that partially fades over 2–4 weeks as it stops being new. The honest next step isn't "ship and move on" — it's ship, then hold Credit Engagement Rate under continued monitoring for a few weeks before treating this result as the final word.

What People Actually Do With a Credit Score They Don't Understand

A qualitative research synthesis exploring why the Before experience's credit section wasn't working — and how the resulting themes directly shaped the redesign visible in the Encouraging Credit Transparency case study. This piece exists to demonstrate qualitative research methodology and synthesis specifically; the quantitative half of the same redesign — hypothesis, sample size math, and a significance test — lives in the Experimentation section of the companion piece.
Illustrative Synthesis — Read Before Anything Else

The sessions and personas described on this page did not happen with real participants. This is a methodology demonstration: the research approach (moderated usability testing, semi-structured interviews, affinity mapping) is standard, real qualitative UX research practice, and the themes below are built from well-documented patterns in fintech UX research and behavioural-design literature — but the specific personas, sessions, and quotes are composite and synthesized for this case study, not verbatim transcripts from real individuals. Every quote below is explicitly attributed to a labeled composite persona, never to a real name. This is the same honesty standard as the synthetic member population disclosed on the Aeroplan piece — synthetic where the underlying data has to be, real everywhere the methodology and reasoning can be.

Research Question & Method

Research Question

When someone opens the Credit section of their banking app, do they leave understanding what to do next — or just aware of a number?

Method

Moderated, think-aloud usability sessions on the existing (Before) credit experience — locate your score, explain what it means, describe what you'd do next — each followed by a short semi-structured interview probing how the experience felt and what participants wished the app had told them.

6 composite sessions · ~20 min each · task-based + retrospective probe

Personas

Three composite personas, each synthesized from a common pattern across the sessions — not individual people.
Composite Persona
Amara
8 months into her first credit card
"I know the number's supposed to be good, but I don't actually know what 742 means for anything I'm trying to do."
Represents new-to-credit users who can recite their score but can't translate it into progress toward a goal.
Composite Persona
Devon
Rebuilding credit after a missed-payment period
"Every time I open this I brace for bad news. It doesn't feel like it's on my side."
Represents users in an anxious or recovery-oriented relationship with their credit, sensitive to tone.
Composite Persona
Priya
Mortgage-shopping within the next two years
"I don't want a score, I want to know if I'm mortgage-ready and what's standing in the way."
Represents goal-directed users who want their credit info tied to a specific real-world outcome.

Key Themes — Affinity Mapping

Four clusters, synthesized across the composite sessions above.
A Number Without a Reference Point Isn't Information
Clustered from: Amara, Priya
Participants could recall their score accurately but consistently couldn't translate it into progress toward something they actually cared about — a car, a mortgage, a "good enough" threshold. The number was memorized, not understood.
Composite observation: "I'd pass a quiz on my score. I couldn't tell you if it's actually good for what I need it for."
Dense Reports Read as Gatekeeping, Not Disclosure
Clustered from: Devon, Amara
The bureau-style report — tradeline tables, "FICO® Score 8 (Bankcard Enhanced)," R1/I1/O1 status codes — was technically the participant's own data, but several described feeling like it was written for someone else to read, not for them.
Composite observation: "This looks like it's for the bank, not for me. I don't know what half these codes mean and I'm supposed to."
A Diagnosis Without a Next Step Is a Dead End
Clustered from: Amara, Devon, Priya (all three)
Even participants who understood their score correctly had no sense of what to do this week to move it. A static number, however accurate, read as backward-looking — a report card, not a plan.
Composite observation: "Okay, so it's 742. Now what? What do I actually do today?"
Warning-Only Language Increases Avoidance, Not Correction
Clustered from: Devon, echoed by Amara
Being told "you are behind on payments" with no accompanying guidance made participants want to close the app rather than open it more. Urgency without a path forward read as blame.
Composite observation: "That warning just makes me not want to look. It doesn't tell me what fixes it."

From Research to Redesign

The explicit line from what these sessions surfaced to the specific decisions visible in the After experience.
ThemeBeforeAfter — design decision
A number without a reference point A bare score (742/850) with no framing for what it enables → Milestone ladder (first card → 700 → 760 → mortgage-ready 780+) and score-band icons tied to real products (🚗 auto, 🏢 general, ✈️ premium, 🏆 top tier)
Dense reports read as gatekeeping Bureau-jargon tradeline table (FICO® Score 8 Bankcard Enhanced, R1/I1/O1 codes) → Plain-language Credit Quick View — utilization and on-time-payment stats stated in everyday terms, no bureau codes on the primary surface
Diagnosis without a next step Static score display, no guidance on what changes it → Next-action nudge card: "Pay $200 toward your LOC to drop utilization to ~15% and gain an estimated +8pts" — specific and quantified
Warnings increase avoidance "⚠️ You are behind on credit payments — repeat failure may impact your credit score" → Encouraging, momentum-framed tone ("Keep it up Maya!") with urgency reserved for genuinely actionable moments, not blanket warnings

These four themes are what the Credit Transparency redesign is built on. The resulting hypothesis — that plain language plus a concrete next action increases engagement — was then tested quantitatively in a full A/B test design.

Methodology & Sourcing

Real: the research methods themselves — moderated think-aloud usability testing, semi-structured retrospective interviews, and affinity mapping to synthesize themes across sessions — are standard, real qualitative UX research practice, described accurately here.

Synthetic: the specific sessions, the three composite personas (Amara, Devon, Priya), and every quote attributed to them are composite constructions built to illustrate common, well-documented patterns from fintech UX research and behavioural-design literature — not transcripts from real individuals. No quote on this page should be read as something a real, identifiable person said.

The Retailer That Shouldn't Work — and Why It's the Most Psychologically Sophisticated Consumer Business in the World

Costco makes 2 to 3% retail margin. It sells food at prices below McDonald's. It gives away samples. It requires a paid membership that competitors have tried and failed to replicate. On paper this is a broken business model. In practice it is one of the most efficient consumer businesses in the world — because Costco is not a retailer. It is a lifestyle membership platform engineered entirely around the psychology of winning.

The Business Model

The Model That Shouldn't Work

Conventional retail logic is simple — buy products from suppliers, sell them to consumers at a markup, and extract margin from every square foot of floor space. Every SKU should contribute to the margin. Every aisle should be profitable. Costco violates every one of these principles deliberately and systematically.

The food court sells a hot dog and soda for $1.50 — unchanged since 1985 and well below any comparable fast food competitor. The pharmacy sells medication at near-zero margin. The samples programme gives away product to thousands of shoppers daily. Retail margin sits at 2 to 3% — the equivalent of barely breaking even on every product sold.

And yet Costco has a 93% membership renewal rate, $4.6 billion in annual membership fee revenue, and consistent long-term growth that makes it one of the most valuable retailers in the world. The explanation is that Costco is not in the retail business. It is in the membership business. The retail operation exists entirely to justify renewing the membership.

Retail Revenue — The Decoy
2–3%
Net margin on product sales. Comparable to a grocery store operating near breakeven. Remove the membership fee and the business model collapses. The products are not the product.
Membership Revenue — The Product
$4.6B
Annual membership fee revenue — almost entirely profit. This is the actual business. Everything else exists to justify this number renewing at 93% every year.
Costco does not sell groceries. It sells the feeling of having access to something most consumers don't. The $65 annual fee is the cost of entry to a parallel consumer economy where everything is slightly better than it should be — and the consumer who pays it is psychologically motivated to find the value that proves they were right to do so.
Argument 01

Why Walmart+ Fails Where Costco Wins

The membership model is not unique to Costco. Amazon Prime has over 200 million members globally. Walmart+ launched in 2020. Target Circle has tens of millions of registered users. None of them have replicated Costco's 93% renewal rate. Understanding why requires understanding what each membership actually sells psychologically.

Walmart+
Friction Reduction
Free delivery, fuel discounts, scan-and-go checkout. The membership reduces the effort of shopping at Walmart. The consumer who joins feels like a slightly more efficient shopper. There is no feeling of winning — only the removal of inconvenience.
Convenience play
Target Circle
Discount Access
Percentage discounts on select categories. The membership provides occasional savings on products the consumer was already going to buy. The value is real but transactional rather than experiential. No feeling of belonging to something exclusive.
Transactional play
Costco
Access to Winning
The $65 fee buys access to a parallel consumer economy where a hot dog costs $1.50, bulk pricing beats any competitor per unit, medication is discounted, and exclusive Pokemon TCG bundles appear without warning. The consumer does not feel like they saved money. They feel like they won.
Identity play

The sunk cost psychology of the Costco membership amplifies the winning feeling on every visit. A consumer who paid $65 to enter is motivated to find the value that justifies that fee. Every discounted product, every sample, every $1.50 hot dog is experienced through the frame of "I was right to pay for this." The membership fee activates purchase behaviour rather than suppressing it.

Walmart has the purchasing power to subsidise a $1.50 hot dog. It has not built the experience that makes that hot dog feel like proof of membership value rather than a promotional tactic. The Costco model is replicable in structure but not in trust — and trust took four decades of consistent delivery to build.
Argument 02

The $1.50 Hot Dog Is Not a Food Product

The Costco food court is one of the most studied pricing anomalies in retail. A hot dog and soda for $1.50 since 1985. A whole rotisserie chicken for $4.99 when grocery stores charge $12. All priced below any comparable fast food alternative. The food court operates at near zero or negative margin and Costco maintains those prices deliberately against internal pressure to raise them.

The food court is not a food business. It is the most visible proof point that Costco is on the consumer's side. Every time a member walks past and sees a $1.50 hot dog they receive confirmation that the platform they paid to access is delivering value no rational business logic would predict. The price is the message. The food is the medium.

The traffic implication is equally important. A consumer who is hungry thinks about McDonald's or Subway. They do not think about going to a grocery store. But they think about going to Costco — because the $1.50 hot dog has conditioned the association between hunger and the warehouse. Costco transformed a retail destination into a dining consideration through four decades of consistent pricing.

Costco's CEO has reportedly told his team that raising the hot dog price would be over his dead body. That is not sentiment — it is brand architecture. The $1.50 hot dog proves that Costco's primary loyalty is to the member, not to the margin. A consumer who believes the platform is on their side renews their membership at 93%.
Argument 03

Samples Are Not Generosity. They Are Architecture.

Most grocery stores offer one or two sample stations — tied to a specific promotion, easily ignored. Costco typically operates six to eight simultaneous sample stations across different product categories. From chicken wings to laundry detergent to protein bars. The difference in scale is not a difference in generosity. It is a difference in strategic intent.

Entry Effect
Consumer enters with a specific shopping list. Within minutes they encounter the first sample station. The shopping mission begins to expand before a single list item is found.
Mission expansion
Endowment Effect
Consumer tastes a product they had no intention of buying. Having experienced it, not buying it now feels like giving something up rather than simply not acquiring something new. The barrier to purchase collapses.
Purchase intent created
Dwell Time
Moving between six to eight sample stations adds twenty to forty minutes to the average shopping visit. Every additional minute in a retail environment significantly increases unplanned purchase probability.
Session extension
Destination Effect
A hungry consumer considers Costco as a dining option not just a shopping destination. The samples programme contributes to the same repositioning as the food court — Costco as a place worth visiting independent of a specific shopping need.
Traffic driver
Discovery Yield
Consumer discovers products across categories they would never have sought independently. The consumer who entered with twelve items leaves with sixteen because four were discovered through taste rather than intention.
Category expansion

The behavioural economics term is precise — once a consumer has experienced a product they partially feel like they already own it. Choosing not to buy feels like losing rather than not gaining. Costco engineers this effect across six to eight products per visit simultaneously. The samples programme is dwell time strategy, product discovery architecture, and endowment effect engineering operating in parallel.

Argument 04

Beyond the Warehouse — The Lifestyle Platform

The most analytically interesting dimension of Costco's current strategy is the expansion of membership value beyond the warehouse entirely. Costco now offers Cineplex movie tickets at discounted prices, restaurant gift cards, travel packages, spa experiences, and exclusive collectible product drops — Pokemon TCG bundles and Lego sets not available at any other retailer. None of these are grocery-adjacent. All of them are proof that the $65 membership covers the consumer's entire life.

Costco has engineered membership value touchpoints across every dimension of consumer life. Entertainment, travel, health, vehicle maintenance, collector culture, and grocery — all covered by a single $65 annual fee. The consumer who has used their membership for a vacation, a tire change, their child's birthday Pokemon cards, and their weekly groceries has a relationship with the platform that is effectively impossible to cancel without restructuring significant portions of their consumer life.

The Pokemon TCG exclusive and the bulk olive oil are the same product decision. Both signal insider access. Both create the winning psychology. Both give the consumer a reason to visit that has nothing to do with their original shopping intention. Costco pivoted itself into a lifestyle platform at the cost of a membership — with various tickets for consumers to stay engaged across every dimension of their life.
The Strongest Objection

Can Anyone Replicate This?

The most credible challenge is the scale dependency argument. The bulk pricing, the manufacturer deals, the ability to subsidise the food court — all require negotiating leverage that comes from extraordinary purchasing volume. A smaller retailer attempting to replicate the model cannot extract the same pricing because they do not bring the same volume. The model works because of the scale. The scale exists because the model works.

This objection is correct and does not defeat the argument — it confirms it. The scale dependency is the moat. Walmart has the scale to negotiate comparable pricing and has attempted the membership model through Walmart+. The reason Walmart+ has not achieved Costco's renewal rates is not scale — it is psychological architecture. Walmart has the purchasing power to subsidise a $1.50 hot dog. It has not built the trust that makes that hot dog feel like proof of membership value rather than a promotional tactic.

The Costco model is replicable in structure but not in trust. Trust was built over four decades of consistent proof that the platform delivers what it promises. A new entrant with equivalent scale could replicate the pricing. They cannot replicate the accumulated trust that makes the pricing feel like a gift rather than a loss leader.

Closing Argument

What Costco Teaches Every Product Analyst

01
Sell the membership, justify it with everything else
Costco's actual product is the $65 annual membership. Every other product — groceries, pharmacy, food court, collectibles — exists to justify renewing it. Identify what your actual product is and design everything else around proving it is worth keeping.
02
The psychology of winning beats the psychology of saving
Walmart+ and Target Circle offer savings. Costco offers winning. The consumer who saves feels efficient. The consumer who wins feels special. Membership programmes that create insider access retain at 93%. Programmes that reduce friction retain at 58%. The emotional frame matters more than the financial magnitude.
03
Subsidise the trust signal, not the product
The $1.50 hot dog is a subsidised trust signal not a subsidised food product. Costco loses money on the food court because it proves something about the platform that no advertising could prove as effectively. What is the equivalent for your product — what can you price below rational market levels to prove the platform is on the user's side?
04
Extend the membership touchpoint across the consumer's life
Cineplex tickets, travel packages, Pokemon exclusives — all make the $65 fee feel relevant on a Friday night, a family vacation, and a child's birthday. The membership that the consumer thinks about most frequently is the membership that renews at 93%. Engineer touchpoints outside the core product experience.
Costco pivoted itself into a lifestyle platform at the cost of a membership — with various tickets for consumers to be engaged across every dimension of their life. The hot dog gets them in the door. The bulk pricing fills the cart. The Cineplex tickets make them think about Costco on a Friday night. The Pokemon exclusives bring the collector back next week on a drop day. And the $65 annual fee makes every single one of those experiences feel like proof that they were right to pay it.

The Broadest Funnel in Toy History

How Lego engineered adult audience acquisition across every consumer identity simultaneously

Lego has engineered the broadest consumer funnel in toy history by doing two things simultaneously — building alongside other brands to create Lego-first products that convert existing audiences, and embedding itself into the leisure identities of adult consumers through lifestyle and hobby sets that require no IP at all.

The Setting

A toy company. Three adult acquisition strategies. One funnel.

Walk into any Lego store in 2026 and you are no longer looking at a toy company's product catalogue. You are looking at the most quietly assembled portfolio of cultural collaborations in consumer products history.

Pokémon. F1. Nike. One Piece. Star Trek. Lord of the Rings. Herschel Supply. The Avengers Tower. Hogwarts Castle. A Lego Fortnite survival mode with 200 million registered players on the other side of the partnership. Botanical collections for the home. Flower bouquets that compete with puzzles and model kits on the adult leisure shelf. Bonsai trees. Garden sets.

None of this is accidental. None of it happened overnight. And almost none of it looks like what Lego was twenty years ago.

For most of its history Lego's promise was simple. Here are the bricks, now build whatever you can imagine. Creative freedom was the product. The child was the designer. The set was just the starting point.

The numbers confirm what the store shelves suggest. Lego's revenue has doubled over the past decade, reaching nearly $10 billion in 2023 and kept climbing to $12.9 billion in fiscal 2025, a 12% year on year increase that outpaced the broader toy market's 6% growth. In 2023 when the global toy industry contracted by 7%, Lego grew its consumer sales by 4%. The adult strategy was not just an opportunity. It was the buffer that separated Lego from every competitor struggling with the same market conditions.

In 2025 Lego offered 860 sets, its largest selection ever, ranging from botanical and architectural builds aimed at adults to IP-driven lines including Fortnite and Star Wars. The breadth of that catalogue is not coincidence. It is the visible output of three distinct adult acquisition mechanisms operating simultaneously.

Mechanism 01
IP Licensing as Audience Acquisition
Each collaboration targets a specific adult consumer segment through an existing emotional relationship with a franchise. The IP does the acquisition. Lego does the retention.
Mechanism 02
Lifestyle Sets as Identity Mirrors
Hobby and lifestyle sets target adult consumers through existing leisure identities, with no IP required. The product reflects something the consumer already believes about themselves.
Mechanism 03
Ecosystem Embedding as Beachhead
Lego enters established platforms and ecosystems with something substantial enough to earn a permanent place, converting audiences that retail and licensing could never reach.
Mechanism 01

IP Licensing as Audience Acquisition

Most brand collaborations work on a simple exchange where two brands share audiences and both benefit roughly equally. Lego doesn't operate this way.

When Lego partners with an IP the product is always Lego first. The set is designed, engineered, and sold as a Lego product that happens to feature another brand's characters and worlds. Lego is not putting its logo on someone else's product. It is building something substantial enough to stand entirely on its own. In most cases Lego is the more recognizable brand at the point of purchase. The consumer knows what Lego is. The collaboration is the reason they stop and look.

This asymmetry is not accidental. It is the mechanism.

For some of these audiences the collaboration is reactivation. Pokémon fans who built Lego as children and had drifted away now have a reason to return. For others it is genuine first contact. A Formula 1 viewer who has never thought about Lego as an adult product until the F1 car appeared in their feed. Both outcomes serve the same funnel. The collaboration either brings someone back or brings someone in for the first time.

The IP does the acquisition work. Lego does the retention work. The licensed set is the entry point into a product ecosystem that has nothing to do with the IP. Once the consumer is inside that ecosystem building a Pokémon set, they are one step away from the botanical collection and the F1 car and the Hogwarts Castle. The funnel is already open.

Mechanism 02

Lifestyle Sets as Identity Mirrors

The second half of Lego's adult strategy requires no licensing deal at all.

While the IP collaborations target consumers through existing emotional relationships with franchises, the lifestyle and hobby sets target consumers through something more personal. What they do with their time and how they define themselves outside of work.

Lego identified the generational shift toward hobby culture before most consumer brands recognised it as a market. The pandemic accelerated what was already happening. Adults were investing more time and money into home, craft, and leisure activities that felt meaningful rather than passive. Puzzles sold out. Indoor gardening surged. Model kits found new audiences. The adult leisure market was expanding and Lego was already positioned to capture it.

Hobbies have become an aesthetic. The generation buying Lego's botanical collection is the same generation photographing their reading corners, their plant shelves, their carefully curated desk setups. Being seen as someone with interests, someone who does things, makes things, collects things, carries social currency in a way that passive consumption does not.

Lego understood this before most consumer brands did. A completed Lego botanical set on a shelf communicates something specific about the person who built it. It says they have patience. They have taste. They engage with their leisure time deliberately rather than passively. It separates them from the person who spent the same two hours scrolling.

The product is the signal as much as it is the experience. And Lego has targeted the hobbies and interests that carry the strongest signal value with remarkable precision. Botany, architecture, automotive design, sneaker culture, geography. Each one speaks to a consumer who wants to be seen as someone with depth and curiosity. Each one gives them a physical object that makes that visible to others.

Each of these sets reaches a consumer who was not a Lego buyer. Each one converts that consumer through a passion they already had, not a franchise they already loved. The distinction matters because the retention mechanism is different. The IP buyer stays because they love the IP. The lifestyle buyer stays because they discovered that Lego is a medium for the things they already care about. Both end up in the same funnel. Both become repeat buyers. But Lego earned them through completely different doors.

Mechanism 03

Ecosystem Embedding — The Fortnite Beachhead

The third prong of Lego's adult strategy is the most aggressive and the most instructive.

Lego has had a presence in gaming for decades. The Lego video game franchise, spanning Lego Star Wars, Lego Batman, and Lego Marvel, built a critically acclaimed library of titles that introduced millions of players to the Lego brand through interactive experiences. But those games shared a limitation. They were single player or local co-op experiences that appealed to a specific type of gamer — the same consumer who already had simultaneous affection for the licensed IP and the Lego brand.

The live service multiplayer audience was an entirely different market. And it was a market Lego had never meaningfully reached.

Lego Fortnite changed that.

Fortnite is not just a successful live service game. It is the most successful live service game ever made with over 200 million registered players and a cultural footprint that extends far beyond gaming into music, fashion, and mainstream entertainment. By partnering with Epic Games to create not a skin, not a limited time event, but a permanent dedicated game mode. Lego's first multiplayer RPG survival experience. Lego entered that ecosystem on its own terms.

The distinction from every previous Lego gaming collaboration is critical. Lego Fortnite is not a Fortnite product featuring Lego aesthetics. It is a Lego product living inside Fortnite's infrastructure. The building mechanic, the survival loop, the creative freedom. These are Lego's design philosophy expressed in a live service multiplayer environment for the first time.

Players who had never considered buying a physical Lego set were now spending hours inside a Lego designed experience with their friends. The brand entered the most competitive attention market in gaming and delivered something substantial enough to earn a permanent place in it.

That is not a collaboration. That is a beachhead.

What Fortnite unlocked that IP licensing and lifestyle sets could not is the live service multiplayer consumer — someone who spends their leisure time inside shared online spaces with friends rather than building alone or collecting on a shelf. This audience had no natural entry point into the Lego funnel before Fortnite. Now they have one that meets them exactly where they already are. The three mechanisms together cover every major adult leisure behaviour. The funnel has no meaningful gaps left.

The Strongest Objection

The IP Dependency Risk

The most credible criticism of Lego's adult strategy is straightforward. A company whose growth is substantially driven by licensing agreements is exposed to the cultural health of other companies' IP. Star Wars loses relevance and Lego Star Wars loses relevance with it. A franchise ends, a partnership dissolves, or a fandom fragments and a significant revenue line weakens overnight.

Lego's own leadership has acknowledged this. Former CEO Jørgen Vig Knudstorp stated in a 2016 interview that the company needed to balance licensed themes with proprietary themes to maintain control over its own destiny and avoid becoming too dependent on any single partner or franchise. Critics have gone further, arguing that extensive licensing risks turning Lego into a delivery mechanism for other companies' intellectual property rather than a creative platform in its own right.

The IP dependency criticism is real. It is also precisely what Mechanism 02 exists to answer.

The lifestyle and hobby sets, the botanical collection, the architectural builds, the iconic cars, require no licensing agreement. They are owned entirely by Lego. They prove that Lego does not need another brand's IP to acquire and retain adult consumers. If Star Wars lost its cultural relevance tomorrow the botanical collection would still sell. The bonsai tree has no dependency on Disney's content calendar.

The three mechanism strategy is self-reinforcing precisely because it includes Mechanism 02. IP licensing is the highest velocity acquisition tool but it carries dependency risk. Lifestyle sets are the slower acquisition tool but they carry no dependency at all. Ecosystem embedding sits between them with high reach and high velocity but dependent on the platform partner's continued relevance. Together the three mechanisms hedge each other. No single failure point collapses the adult funnel.

The IP dependency risk assumes licensing is Lego's strategy. It isn't. Licensing is one of three strategies and the one most easily replaced if the IP environment shifts.

Closing Argument

The Lego First Policy

Lego's adult pivot is not a story about a toy company getting lucky with licensing deals. It is a story about a brand that understood consumer identity more clearly than most dedicated consumer psychology firms and built a product strategy around that understanding with remarkable discipline.

Every IP collaboration targets a specific audience through an existing emotional relationship. Every lifestyle and hobby set targets a specific consumer through an existing leisure identity. Every ecosystem embedding targets a specific platform audience through an experience substantial enough to earn their time. Three mechanisms. Three different types of adult consumer. All feeding the same funnel.

What Makes It Work
The Lego First Policy
No collaboration diminishes the product. No partnership makes Lego feel like a secondary brand. No ecosystem entry feels like an advertisement. When Lego enters a market adjacent to itself it arrives with something substantial. A full game mode. Five exclusive sets featuring fan favourite characters. A botanical collection that competes with craft culture on its own terms. The consumer encounters Lego as a primary experience not a borrowed one.
The Broader Lesson
Precision Audience Acquisition
Most consumer brands understand licensing as diversification. Lego understands it as precision audience acquisition. Most consumer brands understand lifestyle products as category expansion. Lego understands them as identity mirrors. Products that reflect something the consumer already believes about themselves back at them in a new medium.

The most instructive thing about Lego's strategy is what it reveals about consumer identity itself. People do not buy products. They buy expressions of who they are, who they want to be, and what they want others to see. Lego identified that truth across every adult consumer segment simultaneously and built a product for each expression. That is not brand strategy. That is consumer psychology at scale.

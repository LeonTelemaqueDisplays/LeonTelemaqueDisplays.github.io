Brand or Commodity

How Private Label Exposed the Difference — and Why Most Brands Cannot Win

Private label did not create a new competitive threat. It revealed one that was always there. Every national brand sits in one of two positions — brand or commodity. Brands justify their premium through visibility, identity, and signalling. Commodities justify their premium through habit, inertia, and distribution. When private label achieves quality parity, commodities lose immediately. Brands hold. The distinction between them was never about the product. It was always about what the product means to the consumer who buys it.

The Market

Private Label Is Not Winning. It Is Revealing.

Between 2018 and 2025 private label retail sales grew 47% globally — from a position of being perceived as the inferior choice to one where 53% of Canadian consumers believe store brands are as good as national brands. In 2024 every single Canadian household purchased at least one private label product. Private label dollar sales in the US grew at nearly triple the rate of national brands in 2025. The category shift is structural, not cyclical — it began with inflation pressure and has continued after that pressure eased because the quality gap that once protected national brands has largely closed.

The standard interpretation of this shift is that private label is winning a price war. That interpretation misses the more important story. Private label did not win by becoming cheaper. It won by becoming good enough — and in doing so exposed which national brands were premium products and which were premium-priced commodities. The brands that survived the shift were the ones whose premium was always justified by something more than the absence of a cheaper alternative. The brands that lost were the ones whose premium was justified by nothing except the consumer's lack of awareness that a cheaper alternative existed.

The binary is not about product quality. Heinz ketchup and No Name ketchup are made from similar ingredients. The gap is in what the brand means to the consumer at the point of purchase and the point of consumption. Heinz ketchup on the table at a dinner party communicates something. No Name ketchup in the same position communicates something different. That communication gap is the premium and it survives quality parity because it was never about taste.

Argument 01

The Three Variables That Determine Who Survives

Private label vulnerability is not random. It follows a consistent pattern across every category where store brands have gained significant share. Three variables determine whether a national brand's premium is defensible once quality parity is achieved — and national brands that score low on all three are structurally exposed regardless of their marketing spend or distribution advantage.

01
Quality Perceivability
Can the consumer reliably detect a meaningful quality difference in normal use? This is not about whether a difference exists in a laboratory. It is about whether it exists in the consumer's actual experience of the product.
Protected: Heinz — the specific taste profile is the brand. Consumers can identify it blind.
Vulnerable: Selection ginger ale — carbonation, sweetness, ginger flavour. Indistinguishable from Canada Dry in normal consumption.
02
Social Visibility
Is the brand seen by others at the point of consumption? Products consumed publicly or served to guests carry a social signal. Products consumed privately carry none. The social visibility of the brand at consumption determines whether the brand choice is an act of communication.
Protected: Ketchup bottle on the table. Coffee served to guests. Cola at a party. The brand is seen.
Vulnerable: Pasta boiling in the kitchen. Seasoning added during cooking. Ginger ale mixed into a drink. The brand disappears before the product is experienced.
03
Identity Signalling
Does the brand choice communicate something meaningful about the consumer who makes it? Identity signalling does not require the product to be publicly visible — it operates at the level of self-concept. The consumer who buys Tide does not just want clean clothes. They want to be the kind of household that buys Tide.
Protected: Tide — signals a household that prioritises quality in domestic care. Hellmann's — signals culinary standards. The identity is real even when the product is private.
Vulnerable: Frozen vegetables, canned soup, pasta. No identity signal attached. The consumer who buys No Name frozen peas is identical in self-concept to the consumer who buys Green Giant.
The three variable framework explains a paradox that confuses most CPG analysts. Tide charges a 40% premium over store brand detergent. Consumers pay it consistently even when they cannot tell the difference in a blind wash test. The premium is not being paid for cleaner clothes. It is being paid for the identity of the household that uses Tide — which private label cannot replicate regardless of its formulation.

Argument 03

The Structural Trap — Why Most Brands Cannot Win

The honest conclusion of the private label analysis is not that national brands are losing a price war they could win with better strategy. It is that national brands in low-visibility, low-identity categories were never in a defensible position once private label achieved quality parity — and quality parity was always inevitable.

Private label manufacturers have structural cost advantages that national brands cannot overcome. They do not pay for national advertising. They do not carry the overhead of brand management teams, celebrity endorsements, or agency relationships. In many cases they use the same contract manufacturers and the same ingredient suppliers as the national brands they compete with. The quality gap was never infinite — it was a function of investment levels, and as private label investment increased the gap closed.

The national brand in a low-visibility category faces an unwinnable long game. They can invest in marketing to maintain awareness but awareness without a signal worth paying for does not generate premium willingness. They can invest in product quality but private label will match it within a development cycle. The only path to a defensible premium is to build the visibility and identity signals that make the brand worth paying for independently of the product — and most low-visibility categories do not offer that opportunity by structural definition.

This is what the price-conscious ginger ale buyer already understands intuitively. The price difference between Canada Dry and Selection is real. The quality difference is not perceivable in normal consumption. The social visibility of the choice is zero. There is no identity signal attached to ginger ale brand loyalty. The rational decision is Selection every time. And the 53% of Canadians who now believe private label matches national brand quality have reached the same conclusion across dozens of categories simultaneously.

The categories that remain protected — Heinz, Tide, Coca-Cola, Hellmann's — are protected not because they are better products but because they built something that private label cannot replicate. The ketchup bottle on the table. The Tide bottle in the laundry room. The Coke can at the party. These brands built visibility and identity signals so deeply into their consumption occasions that switching requires the consumer to actively choose to communicate something different about themselves. That is the only moat that survives quality parity — and building it requires understanding which occasions offer it before the private label competitor arrives.

The Strongest Objection

Can National Brands Fight Back?

The most credible response to the structural trap argument is that national brands are not passive in the face of private label pressure. P&G, Unilever, and the major CPG companies have developed specific strategies to defend their premium positions — and some of them have been commercially effective. The question is whether those strategies address the underlying vulnerability or merely delay the inevitable in categories where the three variable framework predicts permanent share loss.

What Works
Premium Innovation — Move the Category Ceiling
National brands that launch genuinely differentiated premium products create a new comparison point that private label cannot immediately match. The consumer is no longer choosing between national brand and store brand equivalents — they are choosing between a new innovation and a dated alternative.
Tide Pods over traditional detergent. Hellmann's Avocado Mayo into a subcategory private label had not yet entered. Lay's premium kettle chips creating a new tier above standard potato chips.
What Fails
Marketing Spend Without Signal
Increasing advertising spend in a low-visibility, low-identity category does not address the structural vulnerability. Awareness without a consumption signal worth paying for generates no additional premium willingness. The consumer who cannot tell Selection from Canada Dry will not pay the Canada Dry premium because they saw a television advertisement.
Ragu sauce advertising spend has not protected its share against store brand equivalents. Generic pasta brands have lost category share regardless of marketing investment.
What Works
Category Expansion — Create New Occasions
National brands that create new consumption occasions with higher visibility or identity signalling extend their defensible position into territory private label cannot immediately colonise. The brand grows into its own moat by engineering the occasion rather than defending the existing one.
Gatorade extending into protein bars and recovery drinks — athletic performance occasions with higher identity signalling than standard beverage consumption. Hellmann's recipe partnerships creating a cooking-with-Hellmann's occasion distinct from condiment use.
What Fails
Price Matching — Conceding the Premise
National brands that reduce prices to compete with private label on value confirm the consumer's implicit assumption that the premium was never justified. Once the brand concedes the price argument it has no remaining basis for the premium. The brand becomes the expensive private label.
Several national beverage brands have launched value lines that compete directly with store brands — cannibalising their own premium position without building any new defensible ground.

The defeat of the counterargument is that the strategies which work — premium innovation and category expansion — require the brand to build new visibility and identity signals. They are not defences of the existing position. They are retreats into more defensible territory. The brands that survive private label pressure long-term are the ones that understand they cannot win in categories where the three variable framework predicts permanent vulnerability — and move into categories where they can.

Closing Argument

What Private Label Teaches Every CPG Brand Builder

The private label story is not about value-conscious consumers or inflation pressure or grocery store strategy. It is about the difference between brands that built something worth paying for and brands that charged a premium while hoping consumers never noticed they had a choice.

100% of Canadian households now buy private label. 53% believe it matches national brand quality. Private label sales are growing at triple the rate of national brands. These numbers are not a warning about the future — they are a verdict on the past. The brands that built visibility, identity, and perceivable quality signals have survived. The brands that relied on distribution dominance, consumer inertia, and the absence of a viable alternative have not.

For CPG brand builders at P&G, Unilever, and every major national brand portfolio the framework is actionable and urgent. The question to ask of every product in the portfolio is the same one the private label competitor is already asking: is this brand worth paying for if there is a comparable alternative at 30% less? If the answer is yes the premium is defensible. If the answer requires hesitation the brand is a commodity with a marketing budget — and the clock is running on how long that distinction will remain invisible to the consumer.

Private label did not win by being cheaper. It won by being good enough — and in doing so forced every national brand to answer a question they had been avoiding for decades. Is the premium justified by what the brand means, or only by what the consumer doesn't know yet? The brands built on meaning held. The brands built on ignorance are still losing ground. The private label era did not change what makes a brand valuable. It simply made the answer impossible to ignore.

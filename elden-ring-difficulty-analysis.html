Difficulty as Architecture

Elden Ring · A Three-Layer Framework for Difficulty Design

Elden Ring doesn't have a difficulty curve — it has a difficulty ecosystem. Geographic challenges that reward exploration and patient progression sit alongside pure mechanical skill walls that cannot be bypassed or outleveled. The player chooses which type of difficulty they engage with and when. But the game ensures that true mastery moments remain uncompromising regardless.

I
Layer One

The Linear Difficulty Wall

Traditional Souls games — Dark Souls 3, Bloodborne, Sekiro — are built around a single, non-negotiable philosophy: the wall is the design. Progress is fixed. The path is linear. The next obstacle cannot be avoided, approached from an angle, or outleveled. You either develop the skill to overcome it, or you stop.

This is a complete and coherent design philosophy. It's not a failure of flexibility — it's a deliberate statement. Failure in a linear system sends an unambiguous signal: adapt or quit. There is no geographic alternative. No overlevel strategy. No different path to gather strength and return. The wall exists, and it waits.

Pontiff Sulyvahn in Dark Souls 3 is the definitive example. He sits at the threshold between Irithyll of the Boreal Valley and Anor Londo — halfway through the critical path. The entire game's difficulty architecture converges on that moment. Players who cannot pass Pontiff cannot continue. There is no other way. His arena is a corridor with nowhere to hide. He is a wall, and FromSoft built the entire structure of the game around that wall being unavoidable.

The guidance systems in linear Souls games reflect the same philosophy. Firelink Shrine in Dark Souls 3 and the Hunter's Dream in Bloodborne function as passive waypoints — places to level, upgrade, and commune with the world. Neither tells you where to go. Neither offers a geographic alternative. They are rest stops between walls, not branching opportunities.

What the linear model does well
Creates unambiguous skill gates — progress is earned, not navigated around
Focuses design effort on a single critical path — every obstacle can be precisely tuned
Makes overcoming a boss feel absolute — there is no other way it could have happened
Builds cultural reputation through shared experience — everyone faced the same wall in the same order
What the linear model cannot do
Give players genuine agency over their difficulty experience without external tools
Distinguish between players blocked by mechanical skill versus those who need more context
Sustain engagement for players who hit a wall and cannot currently overcome it
Support multiple simultaneous difficulty philosophies in a single product

This is not a critique of the linear model. It is a documentation of its design contract. FromSoft knew exactly what they were building — and for Dark Souls, Bloodborne, and Sekiro, that contract was exactly right. The wall is the point.

The question Elden Ring asks is: what if you didn't have to pick just one contract?

II
Layer Two

Elden Ring's Difficulty Ecosystem

Elden Ring makes a radical structural decision for a Souls game: the critical path is optional. Melina gives you a destination, never a route. The Lands Between is not a corridor — it is a territory, and you move through it with genuine geographic agency.

This creates something that doesn't exist in any prior FromSoft title: two simultaneous difficulty contracts operating in the same game, at the same time. The player selects which contract they are entering at any given moment. The game never announces which they've chosen. It simply responds to what they've done.

Margit. Stormveil. Early Liurnia. These are gates, but they are negotiable. The player can explore Limgrave, find Torrent, level their character, discover smithing stones, locate better weapons, encounter NPCs who gesture vaguely eastward. A player blocked by Margit can become a player who returns to Margit three levels later with a +3 weapon and wins. This is geographic difficulty: the wall has a workaround, and the workaround is the open world itself.

Malenia, Blade of Miquella. Promised Consort Radahn. These are not negotiable. They are self-selected — located in zones that demand deliberate, sustained effort to reach. And once there, no amount of overleveling, no alternate path, no geographic workaround removes their core mechanical demand. You must learn them. You must adapt. The wall is absolute — and the player chose to stand in front of it.

The open world is not a difficulty reducer. It is a difficulty sorter.
It routes players to the challenge they are currently equipped — emotionally and mechanically — to face.

This is the ecosystem. Not a difficulty curve but a difficulty territory — where geographic challenges and mechanical skill walls coexist, each fully realized, each internally consistent, each serving a different player motivation at different moments of the same playthrough.

Geographic Contract
Margit, the Fell Omen
The first major gate of the Lands Between. Mechanically demanding for early-game players — but the world beyond him is not the only world. Limgrave, Caelid's periphery, the Weeping Peninsula, Stormhill — all accessible. A blocked player has geography available to them.
Overleveling works. Exploration works. The wall is real but not absolute.
Geographic Contract
Godrick the Grafted
Stormveil's lord. Mechanically challenging, but one where gear, summons, and level investment meaningfully narrow the gap. Players who explored thoroughly before approaching feel his difficulty compress in real time — not because the fight changed, but because they prepared.
Preparation transfers. Investment has leverage here.
Mechanical Contract
Malenia, Blade of Miquella
The defining mechanical wall of the entire game. Optional. Self-selected. Located in Haligtree — a location requiring deliberate effort to reach. Lifesteal on every hit. Scarlet Rot phase. A moveset that punishes passive play absolutely. She is not designed to be overcome through preparation. She is designed to be mastered.
No overlevel removes her. No summon trivializes her. The demand is mechanical and uncompromising.
Mechanical Contract
Promised Consort Radahn
The Shadow of the Erdtree final boss. The culminating mechanical challenge of Elden Ring's expanded design. Players who reach him have already demonstrated significant investment. And yet he demands more — precise understanding of spacing, timing, and his attack telegraphs that preparation alone cannot provide.
The endgame of the mechanical contract. Mastery or nothing.
The Elden Ring Ecosystem — Dual Contract Architecture
III
Layer Three

The Design Implication

Most games pick one difficulty philosophy and apply it universally. The linear model or the open model — but not both simultaneously. This is a reasonable instinct. Difficulty inconsistency is a design risk. If a player doesn't understand which contract they've entered, the design feels arbitrary. Progress feels random. The feedback loop breaks.

Elden Ring proved this instinct wrong. You can run multiple difficulty contracts simultaneously without the experience feeling inconsistent — but only if player agency is the mechanism that determines which contract they're in. The design doesn't need to announce which type of difficulty the player is facing. The player's own behavior — where they chose to go, what they chose to pursue — makes that determination legible.

Elden Ring never presents its two contracts as contradictory. Geographic challenges and mechanical skill walls both feel like the same game because they share the same visual language, the same world, the same underlying physics and combat system. The contracts differ in their negotiability — not in their aesthetic coherence. The player intuitively feels the difference between "I could come back stronger" and "I need to be better." They feel it without the game explaining it.

The Closest Parallel

Design Parallel · Ghost of Tsushima
A Dual Difficulty Philosophy in a Different Form

Ghost of Tsushima implements something structurally similar through explicit design rather than implicit geography. Its Lethal difficulty mode targets players who want precise, high-stakes samurai combat — one or two strikes in either direction. Its lower difficulties support the narrative experience without demanding mechanical mastery. Both modes exist in the same world, the same story, the same enemy encounters.

Where Ghost makes the contract explicit (difficulty selection at the menu), Elden Ring makes it implicit (geographic investment and zone choice). Both approaches work. The insight is the same: you can serve multiple player motivations simultaneously if the difficulty system is coherent and player agency mediates the experience.

What Studios Should Steal

01
Separate the Contracts
Don't apply one difficulty philosophy to your entire game. Identify which challenges should be geographic — explorable, levelable, circumnavigable — and which should be mechanical — uncompromising, mastery-gated. Design them differently. Tune them differently. Never confuse them for each other.
02
Let Agency Be the Sorter
The open world doesn't need to announce which contract each challenge belongs to. Player behavior — where they went, what they pursued, how much they explored — will naturally route them toward the challenge appropriate to their current capability. Trust the player to read the signal.
03
Protect the Mechanical Walls
For live-service games and open world titles: if you have challenges designed for mastery, protect them from being trivialized by systems outside their contract. Power creep, seasonal spikes, and gear treadmills that undermine skill-wall content destroy the mechanical contract entirely. Malenia must remain Malenia.
04
Maintain Aesthetic Coherence
Both contracts must feel like the same game. Tonal inconsistency between bypassed content and uncompromising skill walls makes the ecosystem feel arbitrary. In Elden Ring, Margit and Malenia share the same visual language, the same world, the same combat vocabulary — even though one is negotiable and one is not.
The studios building the next generation of open world experiences don't need a difficulty slider.
They need to understand which challenges should be walls and which should be geography.

For live-service environments specifically, this framework maps directly to challenge system design, seasonal content, endgame difficulty tiers, and player segmentation. The question isn't "how hard should our game be?" The question is: which challenges are we asking players to overcome, and which are we asking them to master? They require different design decisions. They require different tuning. And they should never be confused for one another.

The Verdict
Elden Ring's greatest design achievement isn't its world-building or its boss design in isolation. It's that it made two incompatible difficulty philosophies coexist without tension — by making the player the mechanism that resolves them. The open world doesn't make the game easier. It makes the player's relationship to difficulty legible to themselves.
The lone Tarnished beneath the Erdtree isn't small because the world is large. They're small because mastery requires confronting something much larger than yourself — and Elden Ring is the first game to let you choose the scale of that confrontation.

Marathon & the Cost of Misreading Your Audience

A LiveOps Case Study

Marathon didn't fail because it's a bad game. It failed because Bungie underestimated how central PvE is to their own player base's identity — and launched a product that asked a PvE community to become a PvP one overnight.

Layer 01

Good Reviews, Bad Numbers

Marathon is well made. The reviews say so. Players who stick around struggle to find genuine criticism of the gunplay, the visuals, or the core extraction loop. The Steam rating is positive. The discourse around the game's quality is largely favorable.

The player count tells a different story.

88,000 concurrent players at launch. 25,000 a month later. 2,000 to 7,000 fluctuating daily by July. A $250 million game with 1.2 million copies sold generating $55 million in revenue. A free week that spiked numbers back to 41,000 — then watched them collapse within two weeks of ending. A PvE mode that peaked at 14,000 players and trended down within days of launching.

The numbers are not the story of a bad game. They are the story of a game that couldn't answer one question — why should I stay?

The player count, despite the vocal positivity, is the result.
02
Layer 02

The Bungie Player Base Identity Argument

Bungie has never shipped a game without a PvE option. Not once. Destiny's Crucible exists alongside strikes, raids, and story missions. Gambit — Bungie's most direct experiment with forcing PvE and PvP players into the same ecosystem — became one of the most controversial modes in Destiny's history. The invader mechanic pitted casual PvE players against multi-flawless Trials veterans and the community revolted. The forums ran hot for years. Players demanded reworks. The friction was documented, public, and sustained.

But Gambit survived because Destiny gave frustrated players somewhere to go. Got farmed by an invader three times in a row? Return to orbit, load into a strike, run a raid, do some patrol. The PvE ecosystem absorbed the frustration. The player didn't leave the game — they left Gambit and came back when the mood was right.

Destiny · Gambit
A pressure valve built into the ecosystem
Gambit forced the same collision of PvE and PvP identities Marathon now faces — and survived it, because the surrounding game gave players an exit.
  • Frustrated by an invader? Return to orbit
  • Strikes, raids, and patrols absorb the tilt
  • Players leave the mode, not the game
  • They come back to Gambit when the mood is right
The friction was real and sustained for years. The game survived it because the friction had somewhere to go.
Marathon · No Fallback
A sweat fest with no exit
Marathon gave the same PvE-leaning players nothing to fall back on when a session went badly.
  • No strikes. No story missions.
  • No cooperative mode with stakes players could control
  • Just the extraction loop — loot on the line, every session
  • Other players actively hunting you, every run
For the portion of Bungie's audience that plays PvP only when PvE gives them somewhere safe to breathe first, Marathon had no answer.

So they didn't stay. They didn't even try. The game launched as a competitive sweat fest with no pressure release valve — and asked a community that historically uses PvE to decompress from exactly that to simply become a different kind of player overnight.

They didn't. They left.

03
Layer 03

The Vault Breaker Analysis

Vault Breaker told Bungie everything they needed to know. A temporary two week PvE mode with no loot transfer and a deliberately weak starting kit still pulled the game back to nearly half its all-time peak concurrent player count. That number is not a coincidence. That is Bungie's actual audience showing up the moment something resembling their preferred experience existed.

The spike proved the diagnosis. Marathon's retention problem was never the gunplay, the visuals, or the extraction loop. It was the absence of a PvE foundation for the portion of the player base that needs one to engage with PvP at all.

But Bungie made a second mistake after the first. They took Vault Breaker down.

A temporary mode that proved PvE demand was real should have stayed up and been amplified — not removed and replaced with a countdown to September. The correct LiveOps decision was to keep Vault Breaker live, market it aggressively as evidence that Marathon now offers both experiences, and use the momentum it generated to build anticipation for the permanent PvE mode rather than letting that momentum evaporate with the mode itself.

Instead the player count spiked and collapsed within a week of Vault Breaker ending. The same pattern as the free week. Bungie keeps creating windows of interest and then closing them before sustained retention can form.

The permanent PvE mode arriving in Season 3 is the right fix. But arriving in September — six months after launch — into a player base that has already made up its mind about the game is a significantly harder sell than arriving at launch would have been. Bungie is now fighting a reputation problem on top of a design problem. And reputation problems are harder to patch than content gaps.

Layer 04

Intervention Design Document

What the LiveOps response should have looked like
D30 — Data Collection Phase
READ THE DATA
DO NOT INTERVENE YET

The first 30 days are largely outside the LiveOps team's control. Player count will drop in accordance with launch window behavior — what remains after D30 are players engaged with the core retention model. The job at D30 is not intervention. It is reading the data accurately: which segments are staying, which are leaving, what the churn signals look like, what the frustration telemetry says.

The D30 window is where the diagnosis is confirmed — not where the treatment begins.

D60 — The Paired Intervention
RIGHT INSTINCT
WRONG EXECUTION

Bungie took action at D60 with the free week — the right instinct at the wrong execution. The free week ran June 2–11. Vault Breaker launched July 21. Six weeks separated two interventions that should have been deployed simultaneously.

The correct D60 intervention was to pair the free week with Vault Breaker in the same window. The free week removes the access barrier — curious players who never bought in get a chance to try the game. Vault Breaker removes the identity barrier — those same players immediately encounter a PvE experience that speaks to Bungie's actual audience. Together they answer both objections at once. Separately they answer one objection each and watch the same collapse repeat twice.

If Vault Breaker was not ready by D60, the correct contingency was a permanent free-to-play pivot rather than a temporary free week. A $40 price point on a game with a reputation problem and no PvE option was a compounding barrier. Bungie already had the monetization infrastructure — Eververse proved they understand cosmetic economies. Gun skins, Runner Shell cosmetics, VFX on high-tier weapon sets, character customization — all direct translations of what Eververse already did successfully in Destiny. F2P with a cosmetic economy removes the financial friction while the proven revenue model sustains the operation.

Six weeks of separation turned one compounding fix into two isolated spikes.

D90 — The Permanent Foundation
THE RIGHT FIX
ARRIVING TOO LATE, TOO ALONE

By D90 the correct intervention was already planned — the permanent PvE mode arriving in September. The mistake was not the timing of the mode itself but what Bungie did around it. The permanent PvE map should have launched alongside a PvP seasonal reset — giving both audiences a reason to return simultaneously: new content for the extraction players who stayed, a permanent home for the PvE players who left or never came.

Critically — Vault Breaker should never have come down. Keeping it live through the permanent PvE launch would have maintained the momentum it generated rather than letting it evaporate. The D90 window is where the two modes converge: Vault Breaker as the accessible entry point, the permanent PvE map as the destination it was always pointing toward.

The downstream effect of getting D90 right is the most important design argument. PvE players who now have a permanent cooperative home are no longer locked out of Marathon's full experience. They have a pressure release valve. And from that position of safety they become far more likely to explore the extraction loop — not because they're being forced into it, but because they now have somewhere to return to if it goes badly. The same dynamic that made Gambit survivable in Destiny now exists in Marathon.

Bungie's September PvE launch is the right fix. Everything before it was a series of missed opportunities to build toward it rather than waiting for it.

Layer 05

The Framework — Three Principles

01
Vision vs. Direction
A new direction that maintains your core audience's identity while onboarding a new one is sustainable. Abandoning the identity that built your audience in pursuit of a new one is not. The question before any studio launching a new title to an established player base is not "how different can we be" — it is "what contract are we keeping and what are we changing."
02
Pressure Release Valves Are Not Optional
A highly competitive game with no alternative mode creates a high-tension environment that generates anxiety around logging in. Even the most hardcore competitive games — CS2, League of Legends, Valorant — offer modes that reduce the stakes of a session. Without a pressure release valve the game stops feeling like entertainment and starts feeling like a job.
03
When the Solution Is Found, Go All In
A LiveOps intervention that proves demand exists must be committed to permanently, not tested temporarily. Vault Breaker told Bungie exactly what their audience needed. Taking it down after two weeks erased the momentum it built. When the data shows you what works, the correct response is to build on it immediately — not schedule it for six months later and hope the audience waits.
06
Layer 06

Closing Argument

There is always a risk in abandoning your core audience to chase something new. The longer you've been established the higher that risk becomes — because your audience isn't just a player base, it's a community with a decade of identity investment in what you represent. There is a difference between a new direction and dumping your audience.

A Contract Kept vs. a Contract Broken

Digital Extremes is navigating this correctly with Soul Frame. The setting is different. The visual identity is different. The tone is different. But the foundational promise — a PvE-first cooperative looter experience built around player identity and progression — is the same contract Warframe has been honoring for over a decade. Warframe players looking at Soul Frame see a new world built on familiar values. They aren't being asked to become different players. They're being invited somewhere new that still feels like home.

Marathon asked Destiny players to become different players entirely. To abandon the PvE-first identity that defined their relationship with Bungie across two games and a decade of play — and embrace a hardcore competitive extraction experience with no cooperative foundation and no pressure release valve. Some made the switch. Most didn't.

A new direction honors the contract while changing the setting. Abandoning your audience changes the contract and hopes they follow anyway.

Verdict
Bungie built one of gaming's most loyal communities on the promise of heroic cooperative PvE with optional competitive tension. Marathon broke that promise on day one.
The player count, despite the vocal positivity, is the result.
Some made the switch. Most didn't.
